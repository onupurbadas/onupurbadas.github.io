  <p>My current is guided by three central questions:</p>
  <ol>
  <li> (i) How does the representation of historically marginalized groups in local institutions influence environmental outcomes? 
  <li>(ii) Under what conditions do policies aimed at redistributing power lead to substantive changes environmental outcomes?
  <li> and (iii) Reversing the lens: How do environmental stressors shape mental health and well-being?
  </ol>
  <p> Broadly, I care about how environmental, social, and economic systems interact to shape well-being and inequality and how researchers and students engage with these systems. I'm interested in how environmental change, such as natural disasters or the loss of green spaces, affects mental health and how communities adapt to these stressors. I'm curious about how institutional norms shape political behavior and how economics as a discipline filters which questions get asked. I also plan to explore how global consumerism—especially in industries like fashion—drives environmental harm and deepens economic inequality in the regions whose resources sustain it. </p>

<h2>Job Market Paper</h2>

<p><strong>Political Representation, Forest Cover, and Development in Rural India</strong></p>

<p>This paper examines how political representation for historically marginalized communities shapes local economic activity and environmental stewardship. Leveraging the quasi‑random rotation of chairperson reservations for Scheduled Castes (SCs) and Scheduled Tribes (STs) across 5,000 village councils in Karnataka, India, over five election cycles (1994–2010), I estimate causal effects on satellite‑measured night‑time lights and forest cover. SC/ST-led councils see higher local economic activity but lower forest cover, with larger effects under ST leadership. These patterns reveal trade‑offs between development and conservation and point to the need for policies that enable marginalized leaders to pursue development without depleting the natural resources their communities depend on. </p>
